Bank Secrecy Act / Anti-Money Laundering (BSA/AML) Overview
DepositGuard Compliance Knowledge Base

OVERVIEW

The Bank Secrecy Act (BSA), enacted in 1970 and expanded by the USA PATRIOT Act and
subsequent regulation, requires U.S. financial institutions to assist government agencies
in detecting and preventing money laundering, terrorist financing, and other financial
crimes. It is implemented and enforced primarily through FinCEN (Financial Crimes
Enforcement Network) regulations and federal banking-agency examination guidance. A
compliant BSA/AML program rests on five pillars: (1) internal policies, procedures, and
controls; (2) a designated BSA compliance officer; (3) ongoing employee training; (4)
independent testing/audit; and (5) customer due diligence, including beneficial-ownership
identification for legal-entity customers.

SUSPICIOUS ACTIVITY REPORT (SAR) REQUIREMENTS

A bank must file a SAR when it detects a known or suspected violation of federal law, or a
suspicious transaction related to a money-laundering activity or a violation of the BSA,
involving or aggregating at least $5,000 (with no dollar threshold if the institution can
identify a suspect, and different thresholds apply for certain institution types and
insider-abuse cases). Common triggers include:

- Transactions with no apparent lawful business or economic purpose given the customer's
  known profile.
- Attempts to avoid BSA reporting or recordkeeping requirements (see Structuring below).
- Use of an account primarily as a pass-through for funds with no legitimate relationship
  to the account holder's stated purpose (see Mule Accounts below).
- Transactions inconsistent with the customer's expected activity based on their account-
  opening profile (income, stated occupation, expected transaction volume).

SAR FILING TIMELINES

- A SAR must be filed within 30 calendar days of the date of initial detection of facts
  that may constitute a basis for filing.
- If no suspect is identified, the filing deadline may be extended to 60 calendar days
  from initial detection.
- For continuing suspicious activity, institutions should file a follow-up SAR at
  intervals not to exceed 90 calendar days from the date of the previous related filing,
  to keep law enforcement informed of ongoing patterns.
- SARs are confidential; institutions and their staff are prohibited from disclosing to
  the subject of the SAR (or generally to any party) that a SAR has been filed
  ("SAR confidentiality" / anti-tipping-off provisions).

STRUCTURING

Structuring is the practice of breaking a transaction that would otherwise require
reporting (most commonly currency transactions over the $10,000 Currency Transaction
Report, or CTR, threshold) into multiple smaller transactions specifically to evade the
reporting requirement. Structuring is itself a distinct federal crime under 31 U.S.C.
§ 5324, independent of whether the underlying funds are illicit. Indicators include:
multiple deposits or withdrawals just under $10,000 within a short period, deposits split
across multiple branches or accounts on the same day, and rapid, repeated transactions
that appear specifically calibrated to stay under reporting thresholds.

BANK SECRECY ACT THRESHOLDS (KEY REFERENCE POINTS)

- Currency Transaction Report (CTR): required for currency transactions exceeding $10,000
  in a single business day, whether in one transaction or multiple related transactions.
- SAR: no fixed minimum threshold for insider abuse; $5,000 general aggregate threshold
  when a suspect can be identified for most other categories (varies by institution type).
- Monetary Instrument Recordkeeping: banks must record purchases of monetary instruments
  (cashier's checks, money orders, traveler's checks) between $3,000 and $10,000 inclusive,
  purchased with currency.

MULE ACCOUNT DETECTION OBLIGATIONS

Money mule accounts are used to receive and rapidly move illicit funds — proceeds of
fraud, scams, or other crimes — often through a legitimate-looking personal or business
account, sometimes with the account holder's knowledge and sometimes as an unwitting
participant recruited through a job or romance scam. Financial institutions have an
ongoing monitoring obligation (as part of their BSA/AML program) to detect mule-account
patterns, which commonly include:

- A newly opened account receiving multiple incoming transfers from unrelated third
  parties shortly after opening, with no apparent personal or business relationship
  between the sender(s) and account holder.
- Rapid "pass-through" behavior: incoming funds are withdrawn, wired out, or transferred
  to another account/cryptocurrency exchange within hours or a few days of receipt, leaving
  a low residual balance.
- Transaction volume and velocity grossly disproportionate to the account holder's stated
  income, occupation, or account-opening profile.
- Coordinated activity across multiple accounts that share identifying characteristics
  (device fingerprint, IP address, email domain, or beneficiary details), suggestive of a
  fraud ring operating multiple mule accounts.

When these indicators are present, the institution's obligations typically include: filing
a SAR, considering whether to restrict or close the account per its risk-based policies,
and — where the institution suspects the account holder is a knowing participant versus a
victim — coordinating with law enforcement rather than alerting the account holder
directly, consistent with SAR confidentiality requirements.
